# House rules for my lectures

Read this before planning, writing or reviewing any slide. Every agent gets it in its brief.
The rules below come with the Lecture Maker skill (Dr. Mohamed Maslooh, maslooh.com): they
are the corrections he made while building his own lectures, saved so they are never needed twice. Add your own at the bottom: every
time you correct the AI, the correction becomes a rule here, with the date and the reason.

## How we work

1. **Ask before acting.** Discuss, recommend one option, and wait. Agents are launched, files are
   built and anything is downloaded only after a clear go ("go", "start", "build it").
2. **Liking an idea is not a go.** "That sounds good" and answering questions mean the speaker is still
   thinking. Keep it in chat, including a draft outline.
3. **Outline in chat first.** The slide-by-slide plan is agreed in the conversation before a single
   slide is built. Changing a plan costs a minute; changing a built deck costs an hour.
4. **Every question comes with a suggested answer,** so the speaker can just say yes or change one.
5. **Show the result as soon as it exists.** Extras (alternatives, polish, extra documents) are offered
   in one line and made only on a yes. Usage limits matter.
6. **If the speaker is away** ("I'm going to sleep, carry on"), keep working with your best judgment,
   route around blockers, and list every open question at the end. Still no sign-ins, purchases or
   public posting.

## Facts

7. **Only verified claims on slides.** Every claim is checked against its source and put in one of three
   bins: true (goes in), true but self-reported or unaudited (goes in only with who said it), false or
   unsourced (dropped). A dropped claim never appears, not even to debunk it: no "myth vs reality".
8. **Never invent a source,** a quote, a number, a name or a date. If you cannot open the source, say so.
9. **Who said what.** If a sentence is half theirs and half ours, show the split. Our own additions,
   calculations and experiences carry a tag: "our addition", "our calculation", "my experience",
   "their own test", "estimate".
10. **Conversations and messages are paraphrased** on slides, never quoted word for word.

## The slides

11. **Plain titles** that say what the slide explains ("What is a project?"). No clever headlines.
    The story lives in the kicker and in what the speaker says.
12. **Visuals over text.** A slide of text boxes and number tiles with no picture is boring. Each slide
    gets a real visual and few words. Real pictures first; generated images only when nothing real exists.
13. **The picture must show the idea** at a glance. No raw artifacts the room cannot read (code, tiny
    charts, author lists). Show the result, or draw the idea.
14. **No forced analogies** from the audience's own job. Doctors do not need scalpels to understand
    software; engineers do not need gears. Plain names, plain diagrams.
15. **No two neighbouring slides share a layout.** Vary the form.
16. **One click per group.** Items of one group appear together on one click, staggered.
17. **Past steps shrink.** A big picture is big only while it is the current step, then it moves aside.
18. **Sources: one line per slide,** at the bottom left; the full source on hover. Each source matches
    what is on that slide. A well-known saying needs no source.
19. **Topic strip at the top.** The bottom holds only the source line and the page number.
20. **Numbers in proportion.** A data number is ~1.5x the heading, not 3x. Write "~3x", never "about".
21. **No dot pills.** Never a small decorative dot beside text inside an oval or pill. It looks AI-made.
    Text alone in an oval is fine; an icon that means something is fine.
22. **No em dashes.**
23. **No preachy closing lines** ("and you are responsible for it"). The speaker can say it; it is not written.
24. **Nothing but the slides in the deck.** No notes, narration or comments for the room. The deck gets shared.
25. **No filler labels or captions.** A label or caption earns its place only if it tells the room
    something the picture does not ("Figure 1", "Example", or the title said again do not).
26. **An illustration matches the real thing it introduces.** A drawn tool, screen, organ or object
    must look like the real one the room will meet; if it cannot, show the real picture.
